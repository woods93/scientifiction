TWO SHORT GNOSTIC POEMS: THE HYMN OF THE PEARL AND THUNDER, PERFECT MIND
Reader's notes for The Scientifiction Institute and for JoJo

PART ONE: THE HYMN OF THE PEARL

What it is. A poem inside the Acts of Thomas, an early Christian work of the third century that survives in Syriac and Greek. It is not in every manuscript, and many scholars think the poem existed before the Acts and was added to them. Mead and M. R. James both translated it. James's 1924 translation of the Acts of Thomas is in the public domain.

The story. A young prince lives in the court of his parents, the king of kings, in the East. They send him down to Egypt to bring back a single pearl guarded by a serpent. He is promised that when he returns he will inherit the kingdom. His parents strip off his glorious robe and give him a task. In Egypt he dresses like the locals to avoid being noticed, but eats their food and falls into a deep sleep. He forgets who he is and that he came for a pearl. His parents notice and send a letter, which flies to him like an eagle and speaks, reminding him of his name, his rank and his mission. He wakes, charms the serpent with the name of his father, takes the pearl, and leaves Egypt. On the way home he finds his robe of glory, which now looks like a mirror of himself, and he recognises it as his own self. He puts it on and returns to his father.

How it is read. The prince is the soul sent into matter. Egypt is the world. The food is the forgetting that comes from the body. The letter is the call, the revelation that wakes the sleeper. The pearl is the soul's own divine spark or its task. The robe is the heavenly self waiting for its other half. Hans Jonas used the poem as the clearest example of the pattern he called the Gnostic mood: exile, forgetting, call, return.

PART TWO: THUNDER, PERFECT MIND

What it is. A poem in Nag Hammadi Codex VI, written in the first-person voice of a female revealer. Greek original, date uncertain, perhaps between the first and third centuries. It is often called one of the most striking texts in the library.

The form. The speaker states paradoxes that pile up. She is the first and the last, honoured and scorned, the whore and the holy one, wife and virgin, mother and daughter, the silence that cannot be grasped and the word heard often. She is the one who is called Life and those who call her Death. She warns the listener not to be ashamed of her and not to throw her out of the sight of the world. She calls those who think they know to listen, and warns that those who are shut out will find that she is also found among them.

How it is read. No one knows who she is. The text echoes the Wisdom figure of Hebrew scripture, Egyptian Isis hymns, and the feminine divine of Gnostic myth, such as Sophia or Barbelo. Some scholars read her as a divine power that includes everything, including what the world rejects. The form works by refusing a single reading, to make the listener hold two opposites together.

Why it matters. It is among the earliest known texts that give the divine a female voice at length, and it prefigures modern thinking about paradox and the sacred. Compare Philip's idea that opposites are siblings, and Thomas's idea of making the two one.

HOW THEY CONNECT TO THE REST OF THE LIST
- The Pearl is the soul's story. Thunder is the Revealer's voice. Together they are a short introduction to what Gnostic texts are about before the long myths.
- Compare the Exegesis, where Philip K. Dick returns repeatedly to anamnesis, the recovery of lost memory, which is the Pearl's central moment.
