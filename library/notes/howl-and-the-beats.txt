HOWL AND OTHER POEMS (Allen Ginsberg, City Lights, 1956)
A long poem in three parts and a "Footnote," written in long breathing lines. Part One portrays a generation, "the best minds," destroyed by madness, drugs and the machinery of postwar America while searching for something holy. Part Two names Moloch, the devouring god of industry and war. Part Three addresses Carl Solomon, a friend Ginsberg met in a psychiatric hospital. The Footnote answers the poem's despair by declaring everything holy, including the body, the street and the ordinary. The book was seized for obscenity in 1957, and the judge ruled in its favor, a landmark for American free expression.

WHY IT BELONGS NEAR THE GNOSTICS
- It is a visionary text driven by the sense that the world as organized is a prison, and that the holy has been hidden inside the ordinary. That is a Gnostic pattern in a Beat voice.
- Ginsberg's earlier visionary experience in 1948, in which he heard the voice of William Blake, links him directly to Blake on the Hei list.
- The Footnote's insistence that everything is holy is close to the Church's playful sacralizing of the world.

THE WIDER BEATS
- Jack Kerouac, On the Road and The Dharma Bums: the road, Buddhism and spontaneity.
- William S. Burroughs, Naked Lunch and the cut-up method: control systems as language and virus. Burroughs also appears in Dick's glossary and in the dedication of Prometheus Rising.
- Timothy Leary and Ginsberg were friends, and Ginsberg was among the early voices in the psychedelic movement.

CAUTIONS
Part of the Beat mythology was self-made, and some of its heroes behaved badly toward the people around them. Read the work as a vision, not as a guide to living.
