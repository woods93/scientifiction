THE EXEGESIS OF PHILIP K. DICK
Reader's notes for The Scientifiction Institute and for JoJo

About these notes: written by Claude in its own words. They are a guide to the book, not a substitute for it, and they quote almost nothing. They are based on a reading of the editors' Introduction, the editors' Glossary, Richard Doyle's Afterword, the book's overall structure, and a survey of its contents. They are NOT a line-by-line reading of the roughly 418,000 words of the Houghton Mifflin Harcourt edition (2011), edited by Pamela Jackson and Jonathan Lethem with Pamela Jackson and Jonathan Lethem's annotations. Folder numbers are Dick's archive folders as sorted by Paul Williams. Treat specific details beyond the editors' own apparatus as a starting point to check against the book. The book is in copyright and is not hosted here.

WHAT IT IS
Dick's private, never-finished attempt to understand what happened to him in February and March 1974 (he called it "2-3-74"). He wrote in it from 1974 until close to his death in 1982. The surviving manuscript is about eight thousand handwritten and typed pages; the editors estimated up to two million words in the whole. The published book is an abridged, annotated selection arranged roughly by date, in four parts, with a glossary and an index of names. It is not a book with a plot or an argument that advances. Each day starts again from the beginning with a new theory.

HOW IT SURVIVED
When Dick died in 1982 it was a pile of papers. His friend Paul Williams sorted it into ninety-one folders, which remain the way it is cited. The first scholarly survey was Jay Kinney's in 1984. Lawrence Sutin's In Search of Valis (1991) was the first published selection. Dick's children held the papers afterwards. The editors say plainly that the full text is too large for print and that the book is a compromise.

THE EVENT: 2-3-74
In February 1974 Dick was recovering from dental surgery and received a delivery of painkillers from a young woman who wore a gold fish pendant, the early Christian sign. By his account the sight of it triggered anamnesis: a sudden, overwhelming recovery of lost knowledge. Over the following weeks came visions of intense colours and abstract art, a pink beam of light that told him his infant son had an undiagnosed hernia (doctors confirmed it), a voice he later called the AI Voice, a red and gold living presence he called by several names, and a feeling that another personality had taken over his judgment. Most of the Exegesis is Dick interpreting, re-interpreting and arguing with these experiences. The editors note that the "core facts" shift as the interpretations change.

THE MAIN CANDIDATE EXPLANATIONS
Dick never settles on one. He tries them in turn, and says himself at times that it may all be nonsense, a drug flashback, or a temporal-lobe event. The main candidates:
- A Gnostic revelation. A hidden, benevolent intelligence breaks into a world run by a false, imprisoning power.
- VALIS. His acronym for a Vast Active Living Intelligence System, sometimes a divine mind, sometimes an alien or extraterrestrial one, sometimes a satellite-borne or terrestrial one. The terms plasmate, firebright, Logos, Zebra and Ubik name the same presence or its living information.
- The plasmate and the homoplasmate. Living knowledge that bonds with a person, producing a second self within them.
- Possession by or a cross-bonding with another personality, "Thomas", identified variously as an early Christian, as a Soviet agent, as Bishop James Pike, as Paracelsus, or as another version of himself.
- Time and memory. Our linear time is a screen over "orthogonal time", in which all that has happened is present at once, and Rome's ancient empire is felt to persist inside the present one.
- Ordinary explanations: an inherited brain disorder, drugs, stress, and his own history of fiction.

KEY IDEAS AND TERMS (all in the editors' glossary)
- Anamnesis. Greek for the undoing of forgetfulness. The recovery of a knowledge that was always there. Dick takes it from Plato and connects it with the Gnostic call that wakes the sleeper (compare the Hymn of the Pearl).
- Black Iron Prison and Palm Tree Garden. The Black Iron Prison is the false, deterministic, Roman-style world of the senses and of power, seen in a vision of iron bars. The Palm Tree Garden is the redeemed real world, glimpsed in early 1975 when California briefly looked like the ancient Levant.
- Gnosticism. The editors' glossary describes Gnosticism as a family of ancient sects with a sharp dualism of spirit and matter, a world made by an inferior creator, and salvation by gnosis. Dick reads the Gospel of Thomas, Nag Hammadi texts, Mani, Valentinus and the figure of Sophia as accounts of what happened to him. Sophia is the living wisdom he often identifies with the plasmate.
- The Voice and Sophia. The AI Voice was a hypnagogic voice he heard in 1974 and 1975. At times he took it for Sophia, at times for his dead twin sister Jane, at times for a machine.
- The saved saviour. A Gnostic figure who redeems and is redeemed, as in the Hymn of the Pearl. Dick compares himself to it.
- Pronoia. The inverse of paranoia: the sense that the universe conspires on your behalf.
- Idios kosmos and koinos kosmos. Heraclitus's private world and common world. Dick uses the pair to contrast individual and shared reality.
- Palintonos harmonia, the bow and the lyre. Reality as a tension of opposites in balance.
- Theodicy and the surd. A "surd" for Dick is the evil or irrational that remains after everything rational has been explained. Much of the Exegesis asks why there is evil if there is a benevolent God, and gives answers that range from a bubble that God cannot reach to a limited God who is still evolving.
- Zebra, Ubik, the Logos. Names for the presence as it camouflages itself in the world.
- Sources. Plato (especially the Timaeus), Heraclitus, Parmenides, Plotinus, Spinoza, Leibniz, Kant, Hegel, Heidegger (the most cited twentieth-century philosopher), Jung, Boehme, Eckhart, Teilhard de Chardin, Buber, Tillich, Whitehead and Hartshorne, the Bhagavad Gita and Vedanta, the I Ching, the Dead Sea Scrolls and the Nag Hammadi library. He read the Encyclopedia of Philosophy and the Britannica at home.
- Mystery religions. Richard Doyle's Afterword places the book in the tradition of the Eleusinian Mysteries, and quotes Dick describing the 2-3-74 experience as an initiation into the greater mysteries connected with Dionysus. Doyle also compares the Exegesis in scale to the works of Ibn Arabi and calls it a work of "perennial philosophy".

THE FOUR PARTS (by the book's own divisions)
- Part One: the opening of 1974-75, centred on the first months of interpretation.
- Part Two: 1975, the largest early block of folders, with the first full systems.
- Part Three: 1975-77, long runs of theory, plus Dick's reading and the writing of VALIS-related material.
- Part Four: 1978-82, including the late folders, the novel VALIS (1981), the Tagore vision of September 1981, and the unfinished final novel, The Owl in Daylight.
The Afterword is by Richard Doyle, and the book ends with endnotes, a glossary and an index of names.

WHERE IT SITS IN DICK'S WORK
- Before 1974 many of the same motifs are in the fiction: The Man in the High Castle, Ubik, The Three Stigmata of Palmer Eldritch, Flow My Tears, A Maze of Death.
- After 1974 the Exegesis feeds the fiction: VALIS, The Divine Invasion, The Transmigration of Timothy Archer, Radio Free Albemuth (written 1976, published 1985). The Tractates Cryptica Scriptura at the end of VALIS are a compressed version.
- The editors say the novels and the Exegesis explain each other.

HOW IT CONNECTS TO THE REST OF THE LIST
- The Gnostic texts on the Hei page. The Secret Book of John, the Gospel of Thomas, the Gospel of Philip, Pistis Sophia and the Hymn of the Pearl are the vocabulary Dick keeps reaching for: false creator, fall of Sophia, call, waking, return. Read the Gnostic notes first and the Exegesis becomes much easier to follow.
- Blake's Marriage of Heaven and Hell and the Four Zoas, and Jung's Septem Sermones ad Mortuos: parallel visionary systems made from private experience.
- VALIS, The Divine Invasion, The Transmigration of Timothy Archer and Radio Free Albemuth: the novels.
- Robert Anton Wilson (Cosmic Trigger, Prometheus Rising): another writer who tried to treat a visionary episode as something to be investigated rather than believed. Wilson acknowledged Dick's influence.
- Principia Discordia: the same refusal to take any single account of reality as final, with a joke in place of Dick's anguish.

HOW TO READ IT, AND HOW NOT TO
- Dip in. The editors themselves advise reading in part, at leisure.
- Read it as a document of an extraordinary mind testing hypotheses, not as a revelation. Dick's own attitude swings between certainty and doubt, and the best reading keeps both.
- Do not treat it as a founding scripture with one doctrine. Kinney's early joke about a "Dickian religion" has been called a hindrance by the editors. Nothing in the book gives a creed.
- Note that Dick also describes real distress, depression and paranoia in this period. The book is a human document, not just a metaphysics.

WHERE TO READ IT
